Valence bond theory models a bond as overlap of orbitals centered on adjacent atoms. Overlap that builds electron density between the nuclei helps stabilize a bonding interaction. In the hybrid-orbital model, orbitals on **one atom** combine into localized directional orbitals. The number of resulting hybrids equals the number of input orbitals. Bonds and lone pairs can occupy hybrids.

For hypervalent main-group examples, use the stated five- and six-domain hybrid labels for introductory course classification. Those labels alone do not establish substantial valence d-orbital participation in the real molecules. For $sp$, $sp^2$ and $sp^3$, add the superscripts including the implicit one on s to count the number of hybrids. Count central lone pairs in selecting the geometry, and assess each center separately in a multicenter molecule.

### Sigma and pi
A sigma bond has on-axis overlap and no angular nodal plane containing the bond axis. A pi bond typically comes from side-by-side overlap of two parallel unhybridized p orbitals and has a nodal plane containing the axis. Even an antibonding sigma orbital can have a node **between** the nuclei, distinct from a plane containing the axis. Each bonded pair of atoms has one sigma connection. A double bond adds one pi bond and a triple bond adds two in perpendicular planes.

Twisting an alkene’s double bond loses the parallel p overlap, producing a high barrier to rotation. Distinct cis/trans or E/Z isomers can result if each double-bond carbon has two different substituents. Conformations differ through rotation around single bonds and usually interconvert without breaking covalent bonds. Constitutional isomers have different connectivity.
