A temperature change measures the thermal response to energy transfer. For one substance in one phase, $q=mc(T_f-T_i)$. Specific heat $c$ applies per gram; total heat capacity $C$ applies to a whole object or calorimeter. Temperature intervals have the same numerical size in kelvins and degrees Celsius.

### Mixtures and instruments
For an insulated setup, add signed heat transfers and set the sum to zero. A hot metal reaches the same final temperature as cooler water: $m_m c_m(T_f-T_{m,i})+m_w c_w(T_f-T_{w,i})=0$ if the cup absorbs negligible heat. With a non-negligible calorimeter, add $C_\mathrm{cal}\Delta T$. In a coffee cup, $q_\mathrm{rxn}=-(q_\mathrm{solution}+q_\mathrm{cup})$ and the experiment approximates constant pressure. If density and volume are given, compute the mass of all solution being heated. In a bomb calorimeter, use the calibrated assembly heat capacity and $q_\mathrm{rxn}=-C_\mathrm{cal}\Delta T$; its fixed volume gives $\Delta E$ for the sample. Divide by the amount reacted when the question asks per mole. Dissolution can heat or cool the solution, and the same signed balance gives the enthalpy of dissolution per mole.

### Heating curves
For warming within a phase use $mc\Delta T$. For melting and boiling of a pure substance at fixed pressure use $q=n\Delta H_\mathrm{phase}$. Temperature stays at the transition while the two phases coexist; supplied energy changes how the particles interact. Freezing and condensation reverse the corresponding heat signs. For several stages, calculate each separately, keep units consistent and add. Use the specific heat of the phase in each sloped segment.
