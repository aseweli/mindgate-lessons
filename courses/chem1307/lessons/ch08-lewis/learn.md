Atoms and ions interact electrostatically. For charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ separated by distance $r$, Coulomb force magnitude scales as $|q_1q_2|/r^2$. The potential energy scales as $q_1q_2/r$ and is negative for opposite charges relative to separated charges. Ionic bonding is attraction between cations and anions in an extended lattice. Valence-electron transfer is a way to account for ion formation. In a covalent bond, electron density is shared between atoms; in a metallic solid, valence electrons are delocalized across many atoms. Ionic solids tend to be hard, brittle and high-melting. Ions are mobile in the melt, which conducts electricity; they are held in place in a typical ionic solid.

### Draw a Lewis structure
1. Count main-group valence electrons and adjust for charge: add one for each negative charge or subtract one for each positive charge.
2. Choose connectivity. Hydrogen is terminal, and halogens are usually terminal. With common molecules the less electronegative atom often occupies the center.
3. Draw one pair per connection. Complete terminal atoms first, with a duet for H and typically an octet for the others.
4. Place remaining electrons on the center. If it lacks an octet and terminal lone pairs are available, convert lone pairs into additional bonding pairs without adding electrons.
5. Check total electrons, connectivity, octets and formal charges. Bracket a polyatomic ion and give its overall charge.

Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine cannot exceed the octet in these structures. The next lesson handles genuine exceptions elsewhere. Lewis lines represent electron pairs. Count each lone pair as two electrons and each line as two electrons for the whole structure. When checking a local octet, count both electrons of each bond for **each** bonded atom.

### Compare linked species
$\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{NH3}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ each have 10 total electrons and eight valence electrons, with four domains around the center. Their counts of bonded atoms differ, so their molecular shapes differ. $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{H2S}$ and $\ce{H2Se}$ are isovalent, sharing the valence pattern even though their total electron counts differ.
