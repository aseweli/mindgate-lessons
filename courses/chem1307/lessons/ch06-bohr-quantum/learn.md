A continuous spectrum spans a range of wavelengths. Excited gas-phase atoms emit discrete lines as electrons move between allowed energies. Atoms of different elements have different patterns. In hydrogen and hydrogen-like ions, the Bohr expression gives $E_n=-2.179\times10^{-18}Z^2/n^2$ J relative to the ionized state. For hydrogen $Z=1$. The model fits one-electron level energies and gives a poor general description of multielectron atoms.

### Transition workflow
1. Label the starting and ending levels before picking signs. Ground-state hydrogen has $n=1$. A larger $n$ is a higher energy even though the values are negative.
2. Compute $\Delta E_\mathrm{atom}=E_f-E_i$. A move up absorbs energy and has positive $\Delta E$. A move down emits and has negative $\Delta E$.
3. Use $E_\mathrm{photon}=|\Delta E_\mathrm{atom}|$ and $\lambda=hc/E_\mathrm{photon}$. The wavelength is positive.
4. For ionization from level $n$, take the final electron energy as zero, so the required photon threshold energy equals $|E_n|$.

In emission, Lyman ends at $n=1$, Balmer ends at $n=2$ (several visible lines), and Paschen ends at $n=3$ (infrared). Adjacent level spacings get smaller as $n$ increases.

### What replaces fixed orbits
De Broglie links wavelength to momentum: $\lambda=h/p$, or $h/mv$ at nonrelativistic speeds. Heisenberg’s uncertainty relationship uses uncertainties in position and momentum, $\Delta x\Delta p\ge h/(4\pi)$. Improving localization increases the minimum spread in momentum. Schrödinger’s wavefunction $\psi$ describes a quantum state. Its squared magnitude $|\psi|^2$ is probability density; integrate it over a region to get a probability. Orbitals are wavefunctions and their drawings often outline a chosen probability region, while a Bohr orbit is a path in the older model.
