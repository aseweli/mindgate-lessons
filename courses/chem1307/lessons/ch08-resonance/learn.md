Formal charge is an electron bookkeeping assignment. Count all nonbonding electrons on the atom and half the bonding electrons. For an ordinary Lewis drawing this is valence electrons minus lone-pair electrons (and any unpaired electron) minus the sum of bond orders on the atom. Formal charges sum to the species charge. A formal charge differs from a physical partial charge, which reflects unequal electron density.

### Resonance
When more than one valid Lewis drawing has the same atomic skeleton and differing electron placement, the drawings are resonance contributors. The actual electron distribution is delocalized. Moving a bond or lone pair between contributors does not move a nucleus. Equivalent contributors have equal weight. For the three equivalent nitrate contributors, each N–O bond has average order $(2+1+1)/3=4/3$. Within this formal bookkeeping, N is +1 and each oxygen has average formal charge $-2/3$. Those fractions describe the resonance model and do not measure actual partial charges.

For nonequivalent contributors favor complete octets for the second-row atoms where possible, fewer and smaller formal charges, and negative formal charge on the more electronegative atom when the other criteria are comparable. A common exercise is cyanate, $\ce{OCN^-}$ with O–C–N connectivity. Both $\ce{^-O-C#N}$ and $\ce{O=C=N^-}$ obey octets, but placing the negative formal charge on O makes the first a stronger contributor under the usual introductory criteria.

### Octet exceptions
Boron in $\ce{BF3}$ has only six electrons from the three single bonds. A neutral Lewis structure for $\ce{PCl5}$ places ten electrons around P, a third-period atom. These hypervalent Lewis counts are bookkeeping; see Chapter 9 for the course’s hybridization labels. An odd electron count produces a radical, as in $\ce{NO}$ (11 valence electrons) and $\ce{NO2}$ (17). Count the odd electron in the budget and avoid forcing an impossible octet on every atom.
